The 60-second version of the recommendation

You are a full-time police officer with a rotating schedule and a family. That single fact eliminates most of the models in the thread.

Run Model A/B hybrid: a fee-paid car-buying advisor and concierge. Client pays you a flat fee. The dealership sells, finances, papers, and delivers the car. You never take title, never take possession, never touch client money for the vehicle, never sign as a party.
  • Why not licensed broker/wholesaler: premises, bond, plates, inspections, floorplan capital, inventory risk, and a paperwork load that eats the exact hours you're trying to protect.
  • Why not dealer-paid-only: your leverage over the dealer collapses the moment your paycheck comes from them, and disclosure gets messy fast.
  • The one open legal question: whether NJ treats what you're already doing as unlicensed dealer/broker activity. That's the first attorney call. See the Legal chapter.

Front gross vs back gross — and why it matters to you

Front gross = selling price minus dealer cost (invoice, less holdback and factory-to-dealer cash). On a competitive new car this is often a few hundred dollars. Sometimes negative on a volume-target car.

Back gross = finance reserve, rate/MF markup, VSC, GAP, tire & wheel, prepaid maintenance, appearance packages. Frequently larger than front gross.

When you crush the front and leave the back alone, the deal still works for the store. When you crush both and demand a referral fee, you're the broker nobody returns calls to. Pick your battles deliberately.

Holdback: typically ~1–3% of MSRP paid back to the dealer after the sale, brand-dependent. Not yours to ask for by name — but it's why a store can sell at or near invoice and still eat.

Volume / stair-step: hit 40 units, every unit that month pays a bonus. This is why the last three days of the month move pricing more than any argument you'll ever make.

Where dealers genuinely have no room
  • Residual value — set by the captive. Not a dealer lever.
  • Base money factor — set by the captive. The dealer can only mark it up or not.
  • Factory rebates — fixed amounts with fixed eligibility. Stacking rules are the captive's, not the store's.
  • Acquisition fee — bank fee. Sometimes marked up; the base is fixed.
  • Registration / title / state fees — pass-through.
If a salesperson tells you they "can't move" on selling price but "got you a better rate," you're being moved from front gross to back gross. Same money, different pocket.

File 04 / Lease mechanics

Calculate any lease yourself

Four inputs and two lines of math get you within a few dollars of any worksheet in the country.

The formula — memorize this

Depreciation = (Adjusted Cap Cost − Residual) ÷ Term

Rent charge = (Adjusted Cap Cost + Residual) × Money Factor

Base payment = Depreciation + Rent charge

APR equivalent = Money Factor × 2400

Rent charge uses cap plus residual because you're paying interest on the average balance across the term, not the declining balance. That's why paying cash down on a lease saves less than people expect.

Total rent charge over the lease = (Cap + Residual) × MF × Term. That number is the single best way to show a client what a marked-up MF really cost them.

Building the adjusted cap cost correctly

Start at the agreed selling price, then:

  • Add acquisition fee (if capitalized), doc fee, dealer-installed accessories, negative trade equity, capitalized taxes.
  • Subtract cap cost reduction (cash down), positive trade equity, rebates and lease cash applied as cap reduction.

Registration and title are usually paid at signing, not capitalized — but check the worksheet, because rolling them in is common and quietly raises the rent charge.

Watch the shell game: a "$5,000 discount" that reappears as a $1,200 doc/prep fee and a marked-up acquisition fee is a $3,800 discount.
Residual value — what's fixed and what isn't
  • Set by the captive per model/term/mileage, published in the regional bulletin (your Eastern Area Lexus sheets).
  • Applied to MSRP, not selling price. Higher MSRP with the same discount can actually help the payment.
  • Mileage adjustments: dropping from 12k to 10k typically adds ~1% residual; going to 15k typically costs ~2%. Brand-specific — read the bulletin.
  • Some captives residualize certain factory options separately. Rare, but it moves the number.
Residuals change with the model month. A deal quoted August 30 and delivered September 3 is a different deal. Always re-verify against the correct bulletin month.
Money factor, markup, and how to catch it

Buy rate / base MF is what the captive charges the dealer. Dealers may add rate participation — commonly capped by the captive around .00040 (roughly 0.96% APR), brand-dependent.

How to catch it without accusing anyone: ask for base MF and marked-up MF as two separate numbers, in writing, before you talk price. Most managers will just tell you. Then run the markup through the Tools calculator to price it in dollars.

On a $60k car, .00040 of markup over 36 months is roughly $1,400 of rent charge. That's the whole conversation, and it's often easier to win than another $1,000 of front gross.

MSDs (multiple security deposits): some captives let you post refundable deposits to cut the MF — typically 7–9 deposits, each shaving roughly .00007–.00010. Fully refundable at lease end. Excellent for cash-heavy clients; verify availability and count on the current bulletin.

Taxes — NJ and NY are not the same

Practice Commonly applied treatment, verify per deal:

  • New Jersey: for leases longer than 6 months, sales tax is generally calculated on the total of the lease payments at inception and due up front — commonly capitalized into the payment.
  • New York: tax on the total of payments plus any cap reduction, collected up front at signing; also commonly capitalized.
  • Trade credit reduces the taxable amount in both states in most structures — one more reason to confirm trade handling before signing.
Tax rules change and vary by structure. Confirm with the dealer's F&I office and your accountant before you quote a client a number that depends on it.
Reading a worksheet like a desk manager
  1. MSRP — matches the window sticker?
  2. Selling price — what's the actual discount off MSRP, in dollars and %?
  3. Rebates — listed individually? Do they match the program bulletin? Do they stack?
  4. Adjusted cap — does it reconcile line by line? Any fee you didn't agree to?
  5. Residual — % and dollars. Does % × MSRP equal the dollars shown?
  6. MF — is it base or marked up? Ask.
  7. Term and mileage — do they match the residual you were quoted?
  8. Acquisition fee — base amount or padded?
  9. Doc fee — NJ is unregulated and often $500–$900; NY caps it far lower.
  10. Drive-off — first payment, DMV, upfront tax, cap reduction. Anything else needs a name.
Then rebuild the payment yourself in the Tools chapter. If yours and theirs differ by more than a few dollars, one line is wrong — and it's usually the MF or a fee you weren't told about.
Equity, payoffs, and third-party buyouts

Equity = current value − payoff. Positive equity is a down payment. Negative equity gets rolled into cap cost and quietly inflates the rent charge for 36 months.

On negative equity, always show the client the two-line version: "your payment with the negative equity rolled in" vs "your payment if you write a check for it today." Same total cost, very different feel.

Third-party lease buyouts: many captives restrict who may buy out a lease. Before you promise a client their equity, confirm the captive allows an outside dealer to purchase it. Policies shift.

Pull-ahead programs waive remaining payments and are a real, dateable deadline you can use to create urgency honestly.

File 05 / Retail financing

Loans, reserve, and where it's marked up

Buy rate vs contract rate

The lender approves at a buy rate. The dealer may contract the customer at a higher contract rate and keep the difference as reserve, usually capped (commonly 1–2 points, and often a flat dollar cap).

Ask the same way you do on a lease: "What's the buy rate on this approval, and what are you contracting at?"

Reserve is legal and normal. It is also the fastest thousand dollars you can find for a client if the store is willing to move.
Approvals, tiers, LTV, and advance
  • Tiers are score bands with different buy rates. Tier 1 at one captive isn't tier 1 at another.
  • LTV / advance = how much the lender will loan against the vehicle's book value, often 110–140% depending on tier. This is what kills deals with big negative equity.
  • Backend limits: the advance also caps how much VSC/GAP can be financed.
  • Subvented APR is factory-funded and usually can't be combined with the biggest cash rebate. Run both and compare.
Can you get paid on financing?

Law Being compensated for arranging or referring consumer credit is a licensed activity in most states, and lenders generally pay dealers, not third parties.

Do not build a revenue line here. Referral compensation from lenders or brokering credit without licensure is exactly the kind of thing that ends a police career, not just a side business. If you ever want to pursue it, that's an attorney conversation first, not a handshake with an F&I manager.

What's safe: help the client shop their own credit union pre-approval, and let the dealer try to beat it. You get paid your flat fee either way.

The stacking question, asked correctly

Never ask "do these stack?" Ask for the program numbers:

Can you confirm which of these are compatible on the same contract — [Program A], [Program B], and loyalty? And is the subvented MF available with the full lease cash, or only with reduced cash?
For your Ram file: First Responder + Ram Loyalty are commonly compatible, but eligibility documentation and compatibility change monthly. Verify on the current program bulletin before you quote Chris anything.
Timing beats negotiating
  • Month end: volume bonuses land. Last 2–3 days are real.
  • Quarter end (Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec): stair-step targets stack on top of monthly.
  • Program changeover: first days of a new month, before new bulletins are digested, are a bad time to expect aggressive numbers.
  • Aged units: 90+ days on the lot cost the store floorplan interest every day. Ask for the aged list by name.
  • Loaners/demos: mileage discount plus sometimes still eligible for new-car programs. Great value, verify residual treatment.
File 07 / Access

How to get pricing retail can't

Brokers don't get magic pricing. They get fast, clean, repeatable pricing because they cost the store almost nothing to sell to.

What you're actually selling the dealer
  • A customer who is already decided on trim and color.
  • Credit pre-screened, trade documented, payoff in hand.
  • No test drive tour, no four-hour desk battle, no bad CSI survey.
  • Volume toward a stair-step target, on the days they need it.
That's 30 minutes of a manager's time for a unit. Say it out loud when you introduce yourself — it's the entire pitch.
Approaching a new store — the order
  1. Call the store, ask for the GSM or fleet/internet director by title, not by name.
  2. Introduce with volume, not with a request for a discount.
  3. Ask for their preferred format: "How do you want deals sent — text, email, spreadsheet?"
  4. Send the first deal fully built: stock number, program month, structure, client status.
  5. Close one deal at fair gross before you ever ask for aggressive pricing.
  6. After delivery, send a short thank-you and the next opportunity in the same message.
Ask about a broker/fleet sheet on deal three, not deal one. Some groups have them; most just have a manager who decides case by case.
Dealer groups: store, brand, or region?

Start store by store. Group-level relationships come from store-level results, not the other way around. A GSM who likes you will introduce you sideways within the group faster than any cold email to a regional VP.

In NJ/NY/PA/CT you're working around large groups (multi-rooftop operators) plus strong single-point stores. Practical approach:

  • One reliable contact per brand you actually sell — Lexus, Toyota, Ram/Jeep, Honda, BMW, Audi, Subaru.
  • Two backups per brand for inventory and dealer-trade coverage.
  • Track every contact in your dealer database with brand, title, mobile, best contact method, and last deal date.
File 08 / Tactics

Negotiation order of operations

Negotiate in this sequence
  1. Selling price first, in dollars off MSRP. Never in payment.
  2. Money factor — confirm base vs marked up.
  3. Rebates — confirm each one by program name.
  4. Fees — acquisition, doc, prep, accessories.
  5. Trade — only after price is locked.
  6. Payment — last. It's an output, not an input.
The instant you lead with "I need $700 a month," you've handed the desk every variable at once and they'll solve it with term, mileage, or markup.
When to disclose what
Trade existsdisclose early, value late
Payoff amountonly when appraising
Payment targetas a walk-away line, not an opener
Competing quoteonly if real and specific
Your feenever inside dealer negotiation

Disclose the trade exists early so nobody feels ambushed. Hold the value until price is locked so it can't be used to launder a weak discount.

Structuring competitive bids without burning stores

Send the same spec to three stores with a clear deadline and a clear promise: "Best number wins the deal today, and I'll tell you honestly if you lost and by how much." Then actually do it.

Telling a losing manager the winning structure — without naming the store — is why the good ones call you first next month. This is your entire long game.

Walk away when: the store won't put numbers in writing, the fee stack keeps growing, the car isn't actually in stock, or the discount only exists if you finance in-house at a marked-up rate.

Take the deal when: you're within a few hundred dollars of your best realistic target and the car is physically on the ground. Chasing the last $300 has cost more deals than any dealer ever has.

How much gross a store realistically needs

On a competitive volume car: a few hundred front plus whatever backend the client chooses is a survivable deal, especially at month end with a bonus in play. On allocation-constrained or high-demand units: near-MSRP is not the store being greedy, it's supply.

Know which one you're in before you decide the manager is being unreasonable. Ask: "What's your days' supply on this trim?"

File 09 / Compliance

The legal boundary

This is a research summary, not legal advice. Every item here needs confirmation from a licensed NJ/NY attorney and the MVC/DMV before you rely on it.

New Jersey — the question you have to answer first

Law NJ requires a motor vehicle dealer license (N.J.S.A. 39:10-19) to engage in the business of buying, selling, or dealing in motor vehicles. Licensing is administered by the NJ MVC Business Licensing Services Bureau, and licensure carries real infrastructure requirements: an established place of business with an office and display space, a permanent exterior sign, zoning and certificate of occupancy, a surety bond, garage liability insurance, fingerprinting, a site inspection, and a written exam.

The open question: secondary sources describe the NJ dealer license as reaching people who buy, sell, or lease vehicles on their own behalf or on behalf of another entity, with brokers referenced in that scope. NJ does not publish a separate consumer "auto broker" license the way some states do. Whether a fee-paid advisor who never takes title, never takes possession, never handles vehicle funds, and never signs the deal falls inside or outside that definition is exactly the question to bring to an attorney — with your actual contract and workflow in hand.

Best practice Until you have that answer in writing: keep the transaction 100% between client and dealer, take no vehicle deposits, hold no title, never advertise that you "sell" cars, and describe your service as consulting and negotiation on the client's behalf.

Law Separately, the NJ Consumer Fraud Act applies to your advertising and your representations to clients regardless of licensure. Don't promise savings you can't document.

New York — Article 35-B, and it's specific

Law New York defines an Automobile Broker Business under General Business Law Article 35-B as any person who, for a fee or other consideration paid by a consumer, offers or provides a service of purchasing, arranging, assisting, or effecting the purchase of an automobile as agent, broker, or intermediary for a consumer.

  • No license is issued and no application is required. The requirement is a $100,000 surety bond filed with the NY Department of State.
  • Exclusions: persons registered as dealers under Article 16 of the Vehicle & Traffic Law, and any person who acts as agent, broker, or intermediary in the purchase of three or fewer automobiles in a calendar year.
  • Reference: dos.ny.gov → "Automobile Broker."
Practical read: if you take fees from NY consumers for more than three cars a year, plan on the $100k bond. If you stay under four NY deals annually, the exclusion appears to apply as written — but count carefully and confirm the current text, because that's a thin margin to run a business on.

Law Separately, NY requires a dealer license (VTL §415) for anyone in the business of buying/selling vehicles, and NYC adds a DCWP Secondhand Dealer license. Neither applies if you never take title — but they define the line you're staying on the right side of.

The bright lines — do not cross these
  • Law Don't take title to a client's vehicle, or hold vehicles for resale, without a dealer/wholesale license.
  • Law Don't take deposits toward the vehicle purchase price. Your fee is for your service, and it should be invoiced separately.
  • Law Don't sign purchase, lease, or registration documents as a party or as the client's signer.
  • Law Don't accept compensation for arranging financing.
  • Law Don't advertise vehicles for sale, prices, or payments as if you're a seller.
  • Law Don't handle plates, temp tags, or registration on a client's behalf as a business service.
Disclosure: dealer-side referral fees

Practice Bird dog / referral payments from dealers to brokers are common and vary by store, deal, and month.

Best practice Put it in your client agreement in one plain sentence, before the deal starts:

Some dealerships pay a referral fee on transactions I bring them. When I receive one, it is credited against your fee — it never increases what you pay for the vehicle. My compensation is the same either way.
This exact disclosure is also your best sales line. It converts your biggest perceived conflict into proof of transparency, and it matches how you already structure it.
Your other job — the police officer question

Best practice Before any of the above matters, clear the outside-employment side:

  • File the outside employment / secondary employment request your department requires, in writing, and keep the approval.
  • Never use your position, uniform, department email, vehicle, or database access in any part of this business.
  • Be careful marketing to fellow officers in a way that could read as trading on rank or authority.
  • Check your department's policy on accepting anything of value from businesses in your jurisdiction — dealer referral fees deserve a second look through that lens.
A licensing misstep costs money. A conflict-of-interest or outside-employment misstep costs the career. Handle this one first.

Why $895 is the anchor. On a $60k car, a competent negotiation is worth $2,000–$5,000 against what the client would have done alone. $895 is comfortably under 20% of that — easy to justify, easy to say out loud, and it reads as a real professional fee rather than a favor. At 4 deals a month that's roughly $3,600 plus referral fees, on maybe 25 hours. That's the whole business case.
Don't go below $595. Under that, the deal has to close fast and clean to be worth a single evening away from your family, and a cheap fee attracts exactly the clients who take eleven hours. If someone balks at $895, offer the $149 deal review instead — never discount the full service.
Credit any dealer referral fee against these numbers, as you already do. If the store pays $400 on an $895 deal, the client pays $495 and your net is unchanged. That's the "pays for itself" story, told with real math.

The $149 deal review is your defense against free-research mining. It turns "can you just look at this?" into either revenue or a polite no, and it converts to full-service work more often than you'd expect once they see what you catch.

Fee message — the order that works

Flat fee → pays for itself → convenience → referral credit as the kicker. In that order, every time.

It's a flat fee — same number whether you buy a Camry or an LX. In most cases the discount I negotiate covers it and then some, so it's effectively paying for itself. You never sit at a dealership doing the back-and-forth. And if the store pays me a referral fee on your deal, that comes straight off your bill.
Don't promise the fee is "always" covered. "In most cases" is true, defensible, and just as persuasive.

Why quotes get their own tab

Because the value you sell is comparison. Three quotes on three text threads is not a comparison — it's three arguments you'll half-remember next Tuesday.

Enter each quote as a row and the sheet computes adjusted cap cost, depreciation, rent charge, base payment, and APR from the dealer's own numbers. If your payment and their payment disagree, one line on their worksheet is wrong. That's the moment you earn the fee.

It also builds a history. Six months in you'll know which store quotes base MF without being asked and which one always finds $600 of fees at signing. That's worth more than any single deal.
The four habits that keep it alive
  1. Update Status the same day it changes. A stale pipeline is worse than none — you'll trust it, and it'll be wrong.
  2. Never leave a Next Follow-Up date blank on an open deal. That column is your entire follow-up system.
  3. Log hours honestly, including the calls you take in the driveway. It's the only number that tells you which clients to stop taking.
  4. On delivery day, fill the delivery date and set a calendar reminder for month 30 of the lease. That's your repeat business, on autopilot.
Don't buy CRM software. Not yet. A spreadsheet you actually update beats a $40/month CRM you abandon in March — and setup time is the one thing you can't spare.
Keeping it secure
  • Google Drive folder set to private, not "anyone with the link."
  • Two-factor on the Google account. Non-negotiable — this file has client phone numbers and payoff amounts in it.
  • Separate business account from your personal one.
  • Never store SSN, DOB, license numbers, or account numbers. If it's not in the tracker, it can't leak from the tracker.
  • Photos of worksheets and trades: keep them in a dated Drive folder per Deal ID, not scattered in your camera roll.

Client intake form — the fields

Client: name, mobile, email, city/state, best contact time, how they found you.

Vehicle: make, model, trim, must-have options, acceptable colors, deal-breakers, new/used, lease or finance, term, annual miles, target payment, target due-at-signing, flexibility on trim/color.

Trade: year/make/model/trim, VIN, mileage, condition notes, accidents, tires/brakes, keys, lease or loan, lender, payoff, payoff good-through date, current payment, months remaining.

Financial: self-reported credit band (excellent / good / fair / rebuilding), cash available, co-signer yes/no, employment stability yes/no.

Timeline: needed by, current lease maturity date, registration state, insurance carrier.

Authorization: permission to contact dealers on their behalf, permission to share trade info, e-signature, date.

Do not collect: SSN, date of birth, driver's license number, bank or card numbers, full credit reports, pay stubs, or anything you'd have to secure like a lender would. The dealer collects those directly from the client. Your intake stops at "credit band" — that's all you need to structure a deal, and it keeps you out of data-breach territory entirely.
Client agreement — clause checklist
  • Scope: what you do, and explicitly what you don't (you're not the seller, lender, or dealer).
  • Fee amount, when it's earned, and when it's payable.
  • Refund policy — be specific about what happens if the client walks.
  • Client responsibilities: accurate info, timely responses, own credit application.
  • No guarantee of vehicle availability, pricing, incentives, or financing approval.
  • Trade values are estimates until a dealer physically appraises.
  • Dealer is solely responsible for the vehicle, paperwork, delivery, and warranty.
  • Limitation of liability, capped at the fee paid.
  • Referral fee disclosure and credit.
  • Communication authorization (call/text/email, and to contact dealers as agent for negotiation only).
  • Privacy: what you collect, how long you keep it, that you don't sell it.
  • Cancellation by either party.
  • E-signature validity, governing law, venue.
Attorney review required on: scope of agency, limitation of liability, refund terms, referral-fee disclosure language, and whether the agreement itself implies licensable activity in NJ.

What only you can do — and what shouldn't be you

Only you: dealer relationships, negotiation, deal structuring, final recommendation, the delivery-day phone call.

Templated: intake, quote requests, follow-ups, delivery instructions, review requests.

Automated: intake form, e-signature, invoicing/payment, follow-up reminders, calendar booking.

Eventually delegated: inventory searching, running quote requests to a checklist, data entry, social posting.

Rule for a rotating schedule: batch dealer outreach into two fixed windows a day. Deals don't move faster because you checked your phone eleven times — they move at month end.

ACV (actual cash value) is what the store really thinks it's worth. "Trade allowance" is ACV plus whatever discount they moved from the front end. Always ask which one you're looking at.

Why a wholesaler pays less than CarMax

A wholesaler has to buy it, transport it, recondition it, pay auction fees, and still make a spread — while carrying market risk for every day they own it. That's often $1,500–$3,000 of friction before profit.

CarMax and Carvana buy for their own retail shelves, not for auction. They skip the middle layer, they have national demand data, and they'll sometimes overpay on a unit they know turns fast in their system. That's not irrational — it's a different business model.

Which is why your trade play is bidding, not wholesaling: get 4–6 real offers, take the best, keep a small fee. Zero capital, zero risk.
Standardized trade-bid sheet
TRADE BID REQUEST Year / Make / Model / Trim: VIN: Mileage: Exterior / Interior: Options of note: Title status: clean / branded Carfax: [# owners], [accidents y/n], service history Tires: [%] | Brakes: [%] Keys: [#] Known issues: Warning lights: none / [list] Smoker / pets: no / yes Location: [town, NJ] Payoff: $[____] good through [date] Photos attached (exterior 4 corners, interior, dash w/ odometer + lights on, tires, any damage). Bid good through: [date]. Best number wins.

Send to: your franchise used car managers, 2–3 independent dealers, a local wholesaler, CarMax and Carvana quotes, and an ACV/online bid if you have access.

Should you become a wholesaler? No.

What it takes: dealer or wholesale license, qualifying business premises, bond, garage liability insurance, dealer plates, auction registrations, floorplan or cash, title handling, transport, arbitration exposure, and storage.

A $40,000 car, honestly: buy at $40,000 → transport $300 → auction fees $500–$800 → recon $600–$1,500 → sells at $42,000 → roughly $0–$500 of profit, and that's if the market didn't move against you in the two weeks you owned it.

Verdict: wholesale is a full-time, capital-intensive, inventory-risk business. It is the opposite of what you're optimizing for. Build the bid network instead — same value to the client, none of the exposure.

Referral program that doesn't get complicated

One rule, easy to say: refer someone who closes, get a set amount back — cash or credit toward your next deal. No tiers, no points, no expiration.

Ask twice: on delivery day, and 30 days later when they're still in love with the car. Never in between.

For professional referral partners (realtors, insurance agents), check whether any compensation you pay them creates a licensing or disclosure issue in their field. Some can't accept referral fees at all.

File 16 / Curriculum

Learn it in this order

Ten levels. Don't skip ahead — level 5 doesn't work without level 2.

Quiz yourself

1. MSRP $62,000, residual 58%, adjusted cap $56,000, MF .00215, 36 months. What's the base payment?

Residual = $35,960. Depreciation = (56,000 − 35,960) ÷ 36 = $556.67. Rent = (56,000 + 35,960) × .00215 = $197.71. Base payment ≈ $754.38 before tax.

2. A manager says the MF is .00240 and the base is .00200. On a $58k cap and $36k residual over 36 months, what did that cost the client?

(58,000 + 36,000) × .00040 = $37.60/mo × 36 = $1,353.60. That's usually a bigger win than another $1,000 off the price.

3. Client wants to know why $3,000 down only dropped the payment $95. Explain it in one sentence.

Cash down on a lease only reduces depreciation and a little rent charge — spread over 36 months, $3,000 is about $83 of depreciation plus a small rent savings. And if the car's totaled, that money is gone.

4. A store offers a $6,000 "trade allowance" on a car worth $4,500 ACV. Where did the $1,500 come from?

Out of the front end — they reduced the discount on the new car by $1,500 and moved it onto the trade line so the customer feels good about the trade. Always ask for price and trade as separate numbers.

5. You're paid a $500 referral fee by a dealer on a client's deal. What must happen?

It was disclosed in writing before the deal started, and it's credited against the client's fee. Never disclosed after, never kept quietly on top.

6. Under NY GBL Article 35-B, what triggers the $100,000 bond, and what's the exclusion?

Taking a fee from a consumer to purchase, arrange, assist, or effect the purchase of an automobile as their agent or intermediary triggers it. Excluded: registered dealers, and anyone doing three or fewer automobiles in a calendar year.
